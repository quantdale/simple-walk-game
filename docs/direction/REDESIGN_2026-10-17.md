# Simple Walk Game: Redesign Brief from Michael (Oct 17, 2026, 3:21 PM Asia/Manila)

## Verdict on the current build
Michael rejects the current presentation and direction. The game feels like a storyboard, a text-based simulation, or a visual novel. It shows information *about* a game instead of letting him play one. He doesn't accept the current assets, illustration quality, UI or player-facing experience.

## 1. What he wants
A visually engaging, illustrated, interactive world-restoration game where real-life walking powers progression:
- illustrated game environments with beautiful, coherent assets
- characters and NPCs with recognizable visual identities, portraits and expressions, and short, meaningful dialogue
- environmental animation that visibly reacts to progression
- interactive locations, objects, buildings and landmarks
- a living world with atmosphere, depth and personality
- enjoyable restoration and world-building gameplay, with meaningful choices that affect the world

It should feel like an indie game, not a productivity app, a developer prototype or an illustrated storybook.

## 2. Text must not be the main experience
Avoid this core loop: open a screen, read a long description, press Next, read again, watch a percentage go up, repeat. No visual novel.
Dialogue is welcome, but it has to live inside an interactive world. Example: a character stands near a damaged bridge. You talk to them, pick a restoration goal, and later see the bridge get repaired because you walked. The visuals communicate progress, and dialogue supports gameplay, relationships, world-building and decisions instead of replacing them.

## 3. Drop the current art direction
Procedural-looking art, simple geometric shapes, flat symbolic landmarks, primitive buildings and minimal landscapes are NOT acceptable as final art. Don't keep refining the placeholders. Build a proper game-art pipeline with coherent assets. Consider:
- illustrated, layered backgrounds
- recognizable environment objects and modular asset sets
- character sprites or models, plus portraits with expressions
- animation, either frame-by-frame or skeletal
- environmental effects
- before-and-after building restoration variants
- vegetation growth and ecological change
- lighting and atmosphere
- visual feedback for interactions and finished projects

Choose 2D, 2.5D or stylized 3D on artistic quality, performance, cost and the experience you're aiming for. Don't default to 3D just because the project uses Unity, and favor artistic consistency over complexity.
Use only legitimately licensed or original assets, keep provenance records, and follow the repo's licensing rules. If polished assets can't be produced with the tools available, say so as a blocker. Never pass crude placeholders off as production art.

## 4. Dialogue and characters
Real characters with:
- distinct appearances and personalities
- short, natural conversations with expressive presentation
- reactions to restoration milestones
- dialogue that changes as the world recovers
- optional character discoveries

Avoid heavy exposition. Characters are inhabitants of the world, not text generators. No visual-novel system that keeps interrupting walking-driven progression. Every conversation is skippable and can be revisited, and normal automatic progression never depends on reading long dialogue.

## 5. The world must feel playable
A suitable mix of: tapping or selecting locations, inspecting structures, talking to characters, choosing restoration priorities, discovering new areas, watching construction and ecological change, unlocking world interactions, customizing some environmental elements, and optional exploration. It must still fit the low-attention design and never become a daily chore. Walking in real life stays the main source of resources.

## 6. The visual transformation is the reward
Milestones produce noticeable changes:
- a river fills with water
- trees regrow through visible stages
- ruins become repaired buildings
- animals return
- NPCs start using newly opened paths
- lighting, color, sound and animation change as the region recovers

The player should immediately feel that walking accomplished something. Never substitute paragraphs or stats for these changes.

## 7. Redesign the UI
The UI should feel like part of a game. Home puts the living world first, not a vertical stack of status lines. Dialogue gets an appealing, character-focused presentation. Restoration choices get illustrations, previews and clear consequences. Keep the hierarchy simple, readable and approachable, without crowding screens with buttons and text.

## 8. Validate the art direction first (one-time checkpoint)
Before producing lots of assets, put together a small, coherent visual-design package:
1. two or three substantially different art-direction concepts
2. a representative gameplay screen for each
3. a sample NPC and dialogue interaction
4. a ruined-versus-restored environment comparison
5. a proposed approach for asset production and animation

Present it to Michael for approval before locking in a direction. This is a one-time creative checkpoint, not approval on every detail. After approval, keep developing autonomously within the chosen direction.

## 9. Preserve the engineering foundation
Don't needlessly rewrite working domain logic, offline progression, activity ingestion, save persistence, restoration systems or tests. Build the new interactive presentation on the current Millbrook Valley content. Respect AGENTS.md, identity safeguards, licensing rules, validation requirements and worktree procedures. Spend the effort on the actual game experience these systems are meant to support.

## 10. Definition of success
Michael can launch the game and experience a real illustrated world with recognizable locations and characters. He can interact with it, have short conversations, choose meaningful restoration goals, and see visible environmental change powered by his walking. The world looks materially different as restoration progresses, and the game is enjoyable even without reading long descriptions. If it still looks like a storyboard, a visual novel, a text-heavy simulator or crude procedural art, the redesign has failed. Don't optimize for commit or test counts at the expense of player experience. Make it feel like an actual game.
